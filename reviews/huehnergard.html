What does reading this feel like?

Huehnergard begins each chapter with a grammar lessons, introduces vocabulary, and finally presents some exercises. Both translating to and from Akkadian are exercised, as well as grammatical recitation, and eventually, normalization and cuneiform. Answers to exercises are not included, but are available in a separate volume (see below).

What all is included?

up to date, and lengthy appendicies

What choices does the author make?

Huehnergard makes a few pedagogical choices in presentation that are worth understanding from the outset.

The text is primarily concerned with the Old Babylonion dialect, though an appendix is provided on Standard Akkadian

Words are presented with mimation throughout (libbum rather than libbu). Macrons are used consistently to distinguish word forms, and circumflex accents mark contraction.

Cuneiform is introduced in chapter 9, and each subsequent chapter include a few new signs in its vocabulary. Signs are presented (and exercised) in both their Old Babylonion Cursive and Standard Babylonian forms, though the cursive forms predominate the exercises.

Huehnergard unapologetically embraces an evolutionary, diachronic approach. He posits six "alephs" in Akkadian that evolved away over time, and this philosophical framework permeates his presentation. Rather than presenting diptotic and triptotic nouns differently, for example, Huehnergard instead presents the triptotic declension, then later teaches how to contract vowels so as to collapse the three forms into two. This leads to a modern conceptualization undoubtedly unlike what an ancient would have had, and yet also simplifies and streamlines grammatic rules.

Throughout, the text does a great job of presenting material that builds over time, and of limiting references to later material in earlier grammar. Similarly, exercises are contrained to introduced vocabulary, which is friendly to students but also requires some artificial material.
