Summary

In A New Workbook of Cuneiform Signs, Snell teaches 108 signs, interspered with breif asides on topics such as decipherment and polyvalence, and tested with quizzes.

AuthorDaniel C. Snell
Year2022
PublisherEisenbrauns
CategorySupplement

Details

What does reading this feel like?

This really is a workbook, with plenty of space intended to be written in. Each sign is introduced breifly, then written step-by-step with each step having space for the student to follow along. Thus, even complicated signed are easy to write.

Next, some vocabulary using the new sign is given and exercised. The lesson concludes with a few questions--answers are provided underneath. Only transliteration is tested, but the exercises are real words.

This same pattern is repeated for each new sign, each on its own page. So the student will learn the signs along with built-in exercises to reinforce memory.

Occasionally the pattern is interrupted to introduce a interesting topic, like polyvalance, or to review current progress with a quiz.

What all is included?

Sprinkled across the 108 signs are 12 quizzes and 12 topics. An index is also provided

What choices does the author make?

First and foremost, I find the title unnecessarily ambiguous. Languages like Sumerian, Akkadian, and Hitite draw from a similar pool of signs. Other languages, like Old Persian or Ugaritic, use cuneifom but have their own distinct signs. All of these are considere cuneiform, but the signs this book teaches are from the former camp rather than the latter. Addtionally, most vocabulary covered in the text is Akkadian. Despite this, Akkdian isn't mentioned in the title, or on the back of the book, which is equally vague. Calling the book "A New Workbook of Akkadian Cuneiform" would have been more accurate and saved a prospective reader from wondering.

The order signs are presented in is an interesting question. Rather than order the signs by shape (as in a dictionary), Snell orders the signs so as to facilitate real word building in the vocabulary. This is a welcome change from the previous edition.

Generally, polyvalent signs are listed with multiple values. While exhaustively listing values may be prohibited, I was suprised to find some common values omitted.

Perhaps the most contraversial choice made by the presentation here is that the text is a workbook in the first place. To be honest, I find myself uncomfortable writing in the book, even though it is designed for this expressed purpose. Instead, I follow along on scratch paper, which works equally as well. In fact, this works so well, I would have preferred if all the writing space were removed from the book and its size condensed. This may seems like an idiosyncratic reaction, but I suspect it's actually quite common, especially for a book at this cost.

For self-study students

I suspect many students, especially those outside a classroom, will question the value of learning to write cuneiform instead of just reading it. Writing may seem especially silly since it will be with pen and paper rather than stylus and clay. At yet, this is exactly my favorite point about this book--it quickly illustrates to the reader how much going through the tactile motions of writing both strengthens memory and makes the language feel real. For me, that argumented didn't make much cognitive sense, but the experience of trying a few pages changed my mind quite quickly.

Going beyond

One challenge with A New Workbook of Cuneiform Signs is that it presents a set of signs chosen by Snell, and ordered by him. To my knowledge, this sign list doesn't match the progression of any Akkadian textbook. So readers will have to adapt this book into whatever curriculum they are following.

Rating

Recommended.

Snell presents each sign in small, bite-sized chunks requiring very little cognitive effort, while the writing exercises are fun. The asides on various topics are straightforward, enjoyable reading. The biggest obstacle a reader might have is fitting the text into a cirriculum not designed for it.
